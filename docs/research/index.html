Research

Two threads run through everything we do: shock waves and compressible flow on one side, multiphase flows made visible by refractive index matching on the other. Both depend on time-resolved measurements of fast events, and on building the experiments that make those measurements possible.

  • Turbulence in round pipes across area changes
  • Shock waves through abrupt area changes
  • Tomo-PTV of buoyancy driven spheres
  • Inertial solids in high-speed pipe flow
  • Seeing the flow right next to a moving body
  • Shock interaction with local constrictions

Compressible flows and shock waves

How moving shock waves reflect, diffract and recover when they meet area changes, constrictions and porous barriers inside ducts, studied in our automated shock tube with high-speed schlieren imaging and large-eddy simulation.

  • Shock waves through abrupt area changes

    A shock crossing a step bends and bounces between the walls before it flattens out again.

  • Shock interaction with local constrictions

    How a shock wave behaves squeezing through a short narrowing.

  • Shock waves and porous media

    A porous layer smooths the pressure behind a shock, so the load on a wall can be predicted.

Multiphase and index-matched flows

Time-resolved, three-dimensional measurements of solids, bubbles and droplets in liquids, made possible by matching the refractive index of the fluid to the walls and particles so that four high-speed cameras can see the whole flow at once.

  • Inertial solids in high-speed pipe flow

    Following solid spheres through fast pipe flow in three dimensions.

  • Turbulence in round pipes across area changes

    A gentle taper costs more pressure than a sharp step. We measured why.

  • Tomo-PTV of buoyancy driven spheres

    We make a rising sphere invisible, then find it again to measure the forces on it.

  • Seeing the flow right next to a moving body

    Measuring the flow right up to a moving surface, where ordinary reconstructions go wrong.

  • Inception of attached cavitation

    Microbubbles trapped near the wall keep restarting cavitation, whatever the flow carries.

  • Oil–water separation and thin water films

    A droplet crossing into oil stays wrapped in a water film far longer than the crossing itself.
